Summary

Most of the intra-hour price movement sits in a few predictable places (§1). Measured as |d|, the distance of a quarter-hour price from its own hourly mean, the first and last quarters of every hour move 2.6× as much as the two middle ones, and almost equally (10.9 and 11.0 against 4.3 and 4.2 EUR/MWh, averaged over the zones). The movement is therefore concentrated at the hour boundaries, which is why the first-iteration CNN gives the outer quarters 1.5× weight in its loss. Across the day it peaks on the solar ramps, in the morning (h9–h10, 08:00–10:00) and the evening (h17–h20, 16:00–20:00, highest at h18 with 14.1), and it is lowest overnight (2.1 at h4): a 6.7× range between the quietest and busiest hours. Weekends are about 25% quieter than midweek. The size of this movement swings 2.4× over the year and can be predicted from the hourly curve (§2), and its shape is driven almost entirely by the ramps to the adjacent hours (§6–§7).

2. Amplitude is strongly heteroscedastic

Amplitude by month — mean |d|, EUR/MWh, averaged over zones

Amplitude swings 2.4× across the year: quietest in December, most volatile in September. Winter is calm; spring through autumn is not.

Crucially, the hourly curve alone predicts this swing. Per-day correlation between mean |hourly ramp| and intra-hour amplitude:

  • Amplitude tracks the ramp, and the ramp-driven shape (§6) is linear in the ramp, so the shape is roughly scale-equivariant. Normalising inputs and targets by day scale is a sound inductive bias.
  • But normalising the residual (what remains after the ramp effect is removed) by day scale gave no improvement. The ramp term already absorbs the scale, and what is left is roughly homoscedastic.

3. The central reframing

The four quarter prices of an hour average to that hour's price. Writing

P(h,q) = H(h) + d(h,q)

turns the whole problem into a statement about d: the four deviations within an hour sum to zero, by definition.

  • The task is to predict a shape, never a level. The hourly level is given and must be kept exactly.
  • Any prediction written as a zero-sum d meets the hourly-average constraint automatically. It needs no rescaling and no penalty term.
  • Subtracting an hour's own mean from its four values projects them onto the zero-sum space in closed form. That repairs, exactly and cheaply, any step that breaks zero-sum (a median, a quantile, a nonlinear transform).

Mean aggregation keeps zero-sum; median does not. A per-slot median profile violates it by 0.83 EUR/MWh on average and by up to 3.28, thousands of times the 1e-4 eligibility tolerance.

“Amplitude” is mean |d|: how far a quarter price sits from its own hourly mean, on average. It is the size of the thing being predicted.

Zone 2 is structurally different. It never goes negative, has a floor of exactly 0.00 (hit 86 times) and a cap of 349.99. The other three zones go down to about −500. Zone 2 is a bounded market, and it is also the least volatile zone and the hardest to predict from the hourly curve (§7).

Negative prices are common and legitimate, at up to 6.3% of observations; Zone 3 alone has 1,108 prices of exactly zero. Predictions should therefore not be clipped at zero: clipping is wrong in itself, and clipping any of an hour's four values shifts that hour's mean and breaks the eligibility constraint.

6. What drives the intra-hour shape

There is no universal sawtooth. Pooled over all hours, the mean deviation at each quarter is small next to its spread (Zone 0, quarter 1: mean +1.03 against a standard deviation of 18.8). A fixed per-quarter offset explains almost nothing.

The neighbouring hours drive the shape. Quarter 1 tracks the incoming hour-over-hour ramp and quarter 4 tracks the outgoing ramp. Prices drift toward the neighbouring hour's level before the boundary. The auction clears in hourly steps, but demand moves continuously.

The response is asymmetric. Regressing quarter 1's deviation on (previous hour − this hour), fitted separately by sign, gives:

ZonePrevious hour abovePrevious hour belowRatio
Zone 00.5750.6501.13
Zone 10.5570.6801.22
Zone 20.2950.4931.67

Both slopes are positive, so quarter 1 is pulled toward the previous hour either way. The below slope is always the larger one, though: prices converge harder toward a cheaper neighbouring hour than toward a dearer one. A model that treats the ramp symmetrically (a parabola through three points, or a plain linear filter on signed differences) cannot express this.

Plain interpolation understates the size of the drift. Scaling the shape of a quadratic through three hourly means to fit the data gives a factor of 1.66–2.44 across zones. Real drift is about twice what interpolation implies.

The adjacent hours carry almost all the signal. Widening from one neighbour to six adds only 1–6% more explained variance. This is the most actionable finding in the study: wider or whole-day context has very little left to find. A neural network given the whole normalised day curve did no better than one given local summaries.

How much can be explained also varies a lot by zone: 0.80 for Zone 0 against 0.49 for Zone 2. About half of Zone 2's intra-hour shape does not depend on its own hourly curve.

8. Temporal structure

Day-to-day autocorrelation — of the 96-point deviation vector, by lag in days

Two things stand out. Decay is slow: still about 0.5 at four weeks, so long trailing windows stay informative. And there is a visible lag-7 bump: every zone except Zone 3 scores higher at 7 days than at 3. Weekly seasonality is real but modest.

Caution: the weekly bump looks exploitable but is small. A trailing window restricted to same-weekday history was tested and did clearly worse, because the sample shrinks faster than the matching helps. Recency beats similarity.

Summary

The first iteration (§1–§7) trained one small CNN per zone on nothing but the day’s 24 raw hourly prices. On Aug–Sep 2026 it reached a mean MAE of 5.11 EUR/MWh, 47% below the flat baseline and within 0.03 EUR/MWh of the earlier hand-engineered CNN, so raw input cost essentially nothing. Zone 2 gained least, and the figures are optimistic because hyperparameters and checkpoints were selected on the reported window.

The eight-fold protocol of the final ensemble puts 115 comparable recipes on one scale, the mean monthly ratio against interpolation (§8). Re-scored there, the zonal CNNs reach 0.797 with reduced features and 0.803 on raw hourly prices only. The best single families sit between 0.723 and 0.728 and the ensemble reaches 0.708; sequence models (S5, S4D, GRU) trail the best linear model by 0.04–0.06. That research ensemble was re-selected in every fold from six experts: the short-history folds leaned on simpler models, and from April on the same three nonlinear experts won every fold, the recipe frozen for delivery (§9). A RevIN normalisation trial on the champion recipe (§10) made point accuracy worse in every month and widened the intervals by 19–30%, so it was rejected.

1. The idea

Earlier CNN variants were fed hand-built profile channels (day-centred level, hourly slope, curvature, a boundary indicator) plus hour and weekday embeddings. This experiment asks how much of that the network needs: what if it sees only the 24 hourly prices, unchanged, in EUR/MWh?

The output side keeps the reframing from the study tab (§3): the network predicts four quarter-hour residuals per hour, and the hour’s own mean is subtracted from them. The hourly-average constraint therefore holds by construction.

P(h,q) = H(h) + s·r(h,q) − meanq s·r(h,q)

r is the network output and s a per-zone residual scale: the mean |quarter − hourly| on the training days only, reused unchanged at validation.

2. Procedure

Training
2025-10-01 → 2026-07-31, 302 days per zone
Validation
2026-08-01 → 2026-09-30, 61 days per zone (held out of training)
Sample
one (day, zone): input 24 × 1 hourly prices → target 24 × 4 quarter prices
Hardware
Apple MPS, about 1–2 s of training per zone
  1. Score month difficulty. For each zone and each of the 12 months, a scorer CNN is trained on the other 11 months and scored on the held-out one, giving 48 leave-one-month-out folds per objective (96 in all). The scorer has hour and weekday embeddings but no month embedding.
  2. Turn difficulty into weights. Within each zone, the Oct–Jul scores are min-max scaled to a difficulty d ∈ [0,1]. The raw weight is 1 + 0.5·d, renormalised to a day-weighted mean of 1. Aug–Sep scores are computed but never used for weights (§5).
  3. Train one CNN per zone. The network is two blocks of Conv1d(k=3) → GELU → Dropout along the 24-hour axis, then a 1×1 Conv1d head with 4 outputs per hour. The outputs are scaled by s and centred per hour.
  4. Loss. MAE weighted by quarter (1.5, 1, 1, 1.5), because the outer quarters carry most of the movement (study §1), and multiplied by the sample’s month weight. AdamW, gradient clipping at 5, batch 64.
  5. Select the checkpoint. Up to 150 epochs with patience 16; the epoch with the lowest plain (unweighted) validation MAE is kept.

Difficulty follows the seasonal amplitude swing from study §2. Winter (Dec–Feb) is easy. October and March are hard for Zones 0, 1 and 3, while Zone 2 climbs steadily through the summer. 7. Cautions

  • The figures are optimistic. The hyperparameters were searched on Aug–Sep, and the checkpoint (best epoch) was picked on Aug–Sep, the same window that is reported. This is a selection score, not a clean holdout.
  • Not comparable to METHODOLOGY.md. The linear model’s 4.09 is a walk-forward score over the whole year, and its holdout is 2026-06-14 → 09-30. Different windows have different amplitude (study §2), so each model is best judged against its own flat baseline.
  • Single seed. The raw-hourly differences against the reduced-feature model (0.07–0.30) are larger than the ablation’s seed spread (standard deviation ≤ 0.03 for all but one variant), but each zone has only one run.
  • No out-of-sample check yet. October 2026 prices are in the repository, but the checkpoints have not been scored on them.
  • Not directly comparable to the final ensemble. These figures are Aug–Sep MAE with selection on the same window. The final ensemble reports full-month tests with selection on the preceding month. The ratios in §8, all scored under one protocol, are the comparable figures.

9. Research ensemble composition

The delivered model (final §2) refits the April–September recipe of the research champion, whose eight-fold scores are reported on the Final ensemble tab. The champion itself was re-selected in every fold from six experts, with blend weights chosen on the first half of the month before each test.

384 new fits on one RTX 5090: 6 families × 2 objectives × 2 learning rates × 2 seeds × 8 folds, each run up to 300 epochs with patience 35 (2D CNN: 450 / 50).

The six experts

ExpertMechanism

Keeping the hourly mean, keeping quantiles ordered

The linear experts’ median head outputs three coefficients a, u, v and turns them into four quarter corrections that sum to zero:

c = [a + u, a − u, −a + v, −a − v]

a moves the early half-hour against the late one, u splits quarters 1 and 2, and v splits 3 and 4. Added to the interpolation anchor, the hour’s mean is unchanged.

For the linear model the head has 43 outputs: the 3 median coefficients and 40 gap parameters, 10 per quarter. Positive transforms and cumulative sums stack five quantiles below q50 and five above it, so quantiles can never cross. The feature-to-parameter map is linear, but the quantile construction is not. Only q50 conserves the hourly mean; the other quantiles are marginal and need not.

Selection, blending and calibration

  1. Fit all six families. Each is fitted with learning rates 0.001 and 0.003 and seeds 17 and 43.
  2. Two objectives. The ordinary arm weights all training targets equally. The ratio arm weights each training month by its own interpolation error and count, which mimics the evaluation metric. No test denominator is ever an input. The champion uses the ratio arm.
  3. Pick within each family. On selection days 1–15, the best of four single fits or two equal-seed averages is chosen by q50 MAE.
  4. Blend. A frozen menu is scored on the same days: a single expert, rich-linear pairs at 0.25 / 0.5 / 0.75, or the equal top 2 / 3 / 4 / 6 experts. The winning weights are global: one set for the whole test month, all zones, hours and quantile levels.
  5. Calibrate. On days 16–end, a widen-only conformal (CQR) correction per zone and quarter sets the 50 / 80 / 90 / 95% intervals, with deterministic nesting.

Part 3Normalisation trial · RevIN

10. RevIN trial

Reversible instance normalisation (RevIN) puts every day on a common scale before the model sees it, then undoes that scaling on the output. The repository adapted it to this constrained residual task:

  • Normalise inputs per day and zone. The known 24-hour curve gives a mean μ and scale s = √(variance + 1). Current and earlier prices enter as (price − μ) / s. The absolute daily mean and std features are kept.
  • Learn one affine per expert. A positive scale γ and a shift β act on the price channels: two extra parameters per expert.
  • Reverse on the output. The predicted corrections, all quantiles included, are divided by γ, multiplied by s and added to the original interpolation anchor. There is no output shift, because the anchor already restores the level.
  • Same loss. Training stays in EUR/MWh with the original month-ratio weights.

Architectures, learning rates, seeds and blend weights stayed frozen; only the stopping epoch was re-selected on days 1–15. That makes these frozen-recipe transfer tests, not new searches. 1. Task and reframing

Each day the four zones’ complete 24-hour hourly curves are known. The four quarter-hour prices inside every hour are to be reconstructed, and their mean must equal the supplied hourly price within 0.0001 EUR/MWh. Observed quarter-hour history may only come from strictly earlier days.

The models do not predict prices directly. They start from mean-corrected linear interpolation and learn a zero-mean correction to it within each hour:

P(h,q) = I(h,q) + c(h,q) − meanq c(h,q)

I interpolates between hourly centres to quarter centres, flat at the day’s edges, then shifts each hour so its four quarters average to the supplied price. c is the learned correction. Subtracting its hourly mean keeps the constraint by construction: the largest hourly-mean error over all exported quarters is 1.1×10−13 EUR/MWh.

Shrinking the history the model sees moves only the learned correction, and removing it raises error by 0.61 EUR/MWh: the corrections draw on past interpolation errors. Removing history also changes the raw band width by 2.27 EUR/MWh on average, so the same history shapes the uncertainty bands as well as the point forecast. Scaling today’s curve moves the forecast by 3.8 EUR/MWh, almost all of it through the interpolation anchor (3.74); the learned correction moves by about 0.3. A scaled curve has no observed counterpart, so no error change is reported for it, and removing all history can put the model outside anything it saw in training.

No feature-group study of the individual frozen experts exists yet. History §9 collects the closest evidence for each of the three families from related models; a study of the frozen experts themselves is prepared but not yet run.

Why the blend beats its members

Blending buys about 2%. The best single families sit between 0.723 and 0.728 (history §8), and the ensemble reaches 0.708. The six research experts (history §9), counting rich linear (0.727) apart from compact linear, land within 0.006 of one another, so the inputs and the residual-around-interpolation framing matter more than the architecture.

Post-hoc diagnostics on the champion’s saved forecasts over the eight folds (results/interpretability_deep_20261017/).

Averaging forecasts lets errors of opposite sign cancel. Each member’s own error, weighted by its blend weight, gives a mean monthly ratio of 0.729; the blend scores 0.708. Cancellation therefore removes 2.85% of the weighted member error, 0.125 EUR/MWh per quarter pooled. The active experts land on opposite sides of the observed price on 21% of quarters. Averaging does not make the blend better than every expert everywhere: it beats all of its members on only about 8% of hours, and it can dilute a sharper expert’s forecast.

8. Cautions

  • A best-observed recipe. fixed_blend_ratio was promoted after the aggregate test scores were seen. The rule that picks a recipe from pre-test data alone scores 0.708892 in this campaign and 0.708371 at best across campaigns. That is close, but it is the honest number.
  • Not a blind test. Every month, September included, was inspected during development. The repository calls these developmental backtests.
  • Dependent folds. The folds share training history, so no confidence intervals or significance claims are made. Gaps of a few thousandths between recipes (history §8) should not be read as real differences.
  • The delivered model has no test of its own. The frozen refit (§2) inherits the research evidence above, but none of these scores were measured on it.
